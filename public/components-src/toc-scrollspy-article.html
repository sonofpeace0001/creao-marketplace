The quiet craft of interface motion

By Imani Clarke · 7 min read

Motion is information

Every animation answers a question the interface cannot ask out loud: where did that go, what just changed, is it safe to keep waiting. When motion fails to answer, it becomes decoration, and decoration ages quickly.

The strongest products use movement the way a good editor uses a comma, almost invisibly, and only where the sentence would otherwise run into itself.

Easing is character

A linear move feels mechanical because nothing in the physical world moves that way. Objects accelerate and settle. A gentle ease-out on arrival, a slightly longer ease-in on exit, and the whole screen starts to feel like it has weight.

If you can name the easing curve without looking, you have probably over-designed it.

Choreography over spectacle

One element moving is a gesture. Five elements moving at once is a crowd. Stagger the supporting cast by a few tens of milliseconds and let the hero land first, so the eye always knows where to rest.

Build a short list of durations, perhaps 120, 240 and 480 milliseconds, and refuse to invent new ones. Consistency reads as quality long before anyone can say why.

Budgets and reduced motion

Animation has a cost on a mid-range phone, and a cost for the person who gets dizzy. Animate transforms and opacity, keep work off the main thread, and honour the reduced motion setting by swapping movement for a quick fade.

None of this makes the product less delightful. It makes the delight dependable, which is the only kind people remember.

Ship it quietly

The final test is simple: turn the sound off, watch a stranger use the product for a minute, and see whether they ever mention the animation. If they do not, but they say it felt fast, you did it right.
